Lecture 1: What Are Options? The Coupon Analogy

Module 1: Foundations | Duration: ~45 minutes

📚 Learning Objectives

  • Understand options as tradable contracts, not promises to exercise
  • Learn the two types: calls (bet on price going up) and puts (bet on price going down)
  • Grasp premium: what you pay upfront, and how it changes as the market moves
  • See the payoff diagrams—the visual map of profit and loss

1. The Coupon Analogy: Your First Mental Model

Imagine you see a coupon: "Buy 10-piece chicken nuggets for ₹100 anytime before March 31st." The coupon is free, but it represents value—if the real price of nuggets rises to ₹300, the coupon is worth ₹200 (you save that much by using it instead of buying at market price).

An options contract works exactly the same way. You buy the coupon (pay a small upfront fee called the premium). The coupon gives you the right—but not the obligation—to buy something at a fixed price (the strike price) before a set date (the expiration date). And just like a coupon, the option's value can change daily based on what the market price is.

Call Option: A coupon to buy an asset at a fixed price. You own this when you believe the asset's price will rise. If prices rise, your coupon becomes more valuable.
Put Option: A coupon to sell an asset at a fixed price. You own this when you believe the asset's price will fall. If prices fall, your coupon becomes more valuable.
Real Example (NIFTY 50 Call):
NIFTY is currently at 24,100. You buy a Call option with a strike price of 24,000 (this is called "in the money" because the strike is below the current price).
You pay a premium of ₹150 per unit. Since the lot size is 65, the total cost = 150 × 65 = ₹9,750.
This coupon expires on September 26th.
If NIFTY rises to 24,500 by expiration, your coupon is worth much more—it's worth at least ₹500 (the difference between the market price 24,500 and your strike 24,000). But you don't have to wait for expiration. You can sell the coupon anytime before then, locking in profit.

2. The Four Key Components of Any Option

Component What It Means NIFTY Example
Underlying Asset The thing the option is for. You don't own this; the option is a bet on its price. NIFTY 50 index at 24,100
Strike Price The price at which you have the right to buy (call) or sell (put). This is fixed for the life of the contract. 24,000
Expiration Date The last day you can use (or trade) the option. After this, the contract is worthless unless exercised. September 26, 2024
Premium The price of the option itself. This is what you pay upfront to buy the option, or what you receive if you sell it. ₹150 per unit (₹9,750 total for 65-unit lot)

3. Premium: The Market Price of the Coupon

This is crucial: the premium is NOT the underlying asset's price. It's the price of the option contract itself, determined by buyers and sellers negotiating on the exchange through the bid-ask spread.

In the real market, there's no single premium. There's a bid price (what buyers are willing to pay) and an ask price (what sellers are willing to accept). When you buy an option, you pay the ask price. When you sell, you get the bid price. Everything in between is negotiation.

Bid-Ask Spread: The gap between the bid and ask prices. The wider the spread, the more it costs you to trade. A liquid option (lots of buyers and sellers) has a tight spread. An illiquid option has a wide spread and is expensive to trade.
Real-World Premium Example (from actual NIFTY 50 options chain):
NIFTY 29 SEP (September 29 expiry) options. Current NIFTY at 23,398.
23,500 Call: Bid = 310, Ask = 320. Last Trade Price = 314.75
If you want to buy this call, you pay the ask = 320 per unit. Cost per lot = 320 × 65 = ₹20,800.
If you want to sell this call, you receive the bid = 310 per unit. Proceeds per lot = 310 × 65 = ₹20,150.
The difference (10 points) is the bid-ask spread. That's the market's transaction cost.

Two hours later, NIFTY rallies to 23,500. The same 23,500 call now has Bid = 340, Ask = 350.
The premium changed because the option is now "in the money" and more valuable. You can sell what you bought for 320 at the new ask of 350, locking in a 30-point profit (₹1,950 per lot).

What moves the bid-ask spread? Two things: (1) How much the underlying asset moves, and (2) How much time is left until expiration. If NIFTY rallies sharply, call premiums increase and put premiums decrease. As expiration approaches, time value decays and options become cheaper (all else equal). But at any given moment, the actual price you pay or receive is the bid or ask—set by real traders in real time, not a formula.

4. How You Trade Options: Buy Low, Sell High

Here's what matters: you do not exercise the option (with rare exceptions). You trade the premium.

Your profit/loss is: (Exit Premium – Entry Premium) × Lot Size

Trading Example (No Exercise):
You buy a NIFTY Call (strike 24,000) for premium 150. Cost = 150 × 65 = ₹9,750.
The next day, NIFTY rallies to 24,300. The premium is now 320 (much more intrinsic value).
You sell the call for premium 320. Sale = 320 × 65 = ₹20,800.
Your profit = ₹20,800 – ₹9,750 = ₹11,050.
You never bought or owned NIFTY. You just traded the option contract itself.

5. Call Options: A Bet on Rising Prices

When you buy a call, you're betting that the underlying asset's price will rise. The higher it goes, the more valuable your call becomes.

When you sell a call (riskier), you're betting the price stays flat or falls. You keep the premium you received.

6. Put Options: A Bet on Falling Prices

When you buy a put, you're betting that the underlying asset's price will fall. The lower it goes, the more valuable your put becomes.

When you sell a put (riskier), you're betting the price stays flat or rises. You keep the premium you received.

7. Selling Options: The Flip Side

Selling (or "writing") an option is the opposite position and carries much higher risk.

Sell a Call: You receive the premium upfront, but your profit is capped at that premium. If NIFTY rises above your strike, your loss is unlimited. You must deposit large margin (~6-10x the strike value) because the exchange needs to protect itself against your potential losses.
Sell a Put: You receive the premium upfront, but your profit is capped at that premium. If NIFTY falls below your strike, your loss grows as the price falls. Again, large margin required.

Margin Asymmetry: When you buy an option, you only pay the premium (e.g., ₹9,750). No additional margin because your risk is defined and limited.

When you sell an option, the exchange requires margin of 6-10x the notional strike value (e.g., 24,000 × 65 units × 6-10% = ₹94,000 to ₹156,000) because your risk is undefined and potentially large.

8. Moneyness: In, At, and Out of the Money

In the Money (ITM): An option that has intrinsic value (it's profitable to exercise right now).
• Call ITM: Current Price > Strike Price
• Put ITM: Current Price < Strike Price
At the Money (ATM): Current Price ≈ Strike Price. The option has no intrinsic value, only time value.
Out of the Money (OTM): An option that has no intrinsic value.
• Call OTM: Current Price < Strike Price
• Put OTM: Current Price > Strike Price
Moneyness Example:
NIFTY at 24,100.
24,000 Call: ITM (intrinsic value = 100)
24,100 Call: ATM (no intrinsic value, all premium is time value)
24,200 Call: OTM (no intrinsic value, premium is purely time value, and it's expensive because traders hope NIFTY will reach or exceed 24,200)

9. Time Decay: The Enemy for Option Buyers

One of the most important concepts: as expiration approaches, extrinsic value (time value) decays toward zero.

For option buyers, this is bad. You pay premium for time value, and if nothing happens, that time value melts away every day.

For option sellers, time decay is a friend. You keep the premium as time value decays.

Time Decay Example:
You buy a NIFTY Call at strike 24,000 for premium 150, with 30 days to expiration.
Intrinsic value = 100 (NIFTY at 24,100)
Time value = 50 (traders pay for 30 days of possibility)
One week later, NIFTY is still at 24,100. Now there are 23 days left.
Time value has decayed to 35 (less time left = less valuable).
Premium is now 100 + 35 = 135.
You paid 150, it's now worth 135. You lost ₹15 × 65 = ₹975 just from time decay, even though NIFTY didn't move.

Click on each question to reveal the answer.

Q1: What is the maximum loss when you buy a call option?

A: The premium you paid upfront. No matter how far the price falls, you cannot lose more than what you invested. For example, if you paid ₹9,750 for a call and NIFTY crashes to zero, your loss is ₹9,750—not more.

Q2: What is the difference between premium and intrinsic value?

A: Premium is the actual market price of the option contract (what buyers and sellers negotiate). Intrinsic value is how much "in the money" it is (Current Price – Strike for a call, Strike – Current Price for a put). Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value. The time value is extra that traders pay for the possibility of future moves.

Q3: If I buy a call option for premium 150 and sell it later for premium 180, what is my profit?

A: Profit per unit = 180 – 150 = 30 points. If the lot size is 65, total profit = 30 × 65 = ₹1,950. You never exercised the option or owned the underlying; you simply traded the premium.

Q4: Why does time decay hurt option buyers but help option sellers?

A: Time value (extrinsic value) decreases as expiration approaches. Buyers paid for this time value upfront, so as it decays, their option becomes cheaper—loss. Sellers received the premium upfront; as time value decays to zero, they keep the premium they received—profit. Time decay is a force working for sellers and against buyers.

Q5: What does it mean for a put option to be "in the money"?

A: A put is ITM when the current price is below the strike price. For example, if NIFTY is at 23,800 and the put strike is 24,000, it's ITM with an intrinsic value of 200 (24,000 – 23,800). This means the put is profitable to exercise right now, but as a trader, you'd sell the premium instead of exercising.
